Cash flow gaps and how small businesses survive them

Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact. Here is how to stop running out of it.

Educational only, not financial advice.

Many small businesses do not fail because they are unprofitable. They fail because the money arrives later than the bills. You can have a record month in sales and still be unable to pay your supplier on Friday. That is a cash flow gap.

Where the gap comes from

  • Receivables: customers, resellers or corporate clients who pay in 30, 60 or 90 days, while your suppliers want cash now.
  • Inventory: money sitting on the shelf as stock that is not moving.
  • Lumpy expenses: rent, permits, 13th month pay and annual renewals that land in one month.
  • Owner withdrawals: taking money from the drawer whenever needed, with no fixed salary.
  • Growth itself: a big order needs more stock and labor before the customer pays.

The 30-day cash flow plan

Week 1: Map

Write down every peso you expect to come in and go out over the next 30 days, by date. A notebook or a simple spreadsheet is enough. Include loan payments, card due dates and your own salary. Mark the days when the balance goes below zero. Those are your gap days.

Week 2: Cut and delay

Look at outflows on or before gap days. Which can be delayed politely, reduced or cancelled? Subscriptions you do not use, stock you can order in smaller batches, expenses you can move to after the customer pays. Ask suppliers for 15 or 30 day terms; many will say yes to a reliable buyer.

Week 3: Collect faster

  • Ask for a deposit (for example 30% to 50%) on custom or big orders.
  • Send invoices the same day you deliver, with QR or bank details on it.
  • Follow up on the due date, not a week after.
  • Offer a small bonus, not a discount, for early payment.
  • Clear slow stock with bundles to turn it back into cash.

Week 4: Build the buffer

Open a separate savings account for your business buffer. Start with a target of one month of fixed costs. Move a fixed percentage of every sale into it, even 5%. Keep it in an insured account that earns something, not in the drawer. See where to park idle money.

Should you borrow to cover the gap?

Sometimes a short bridge is reasonable. The rule: borrow only when you know exactly when and from where the repayment comes, and the cost is smaller than what you lose by not borrowing. A credit card can be a zero-interest bridge if the cash arrives before the due date. If it does not, interest can reach the BSP ceiling of 3% a month. Run the Leverage Reality Check first. Avoid informal "5-6" lending; the effective cost is extremely high.

Three numbers to watch every week

  1. Cash on hand today, across all business accounts.
  2. Money owed to you, and how many days late.
  3. Bills due in the next 14 days.

If cash on hand plus collections you are sure of is less than bills due, you have a gap coming. You now have two weeks to act instead of two hours.

Use a simple tool, not a perfect one

You do not need accounting software to start. A notebook with three columns (date, in, out) and a running balance works. What matters is that you update it every day and look at it every week. Once the habit sticks, a spreadsheet makes the 30-day view easier, and the cheat sheet has a layout you can copy.

Signs you are fixing it

  • You know your gap days before they happen.
  • You stopped paying suppliers late.
  • Your buffer account grows every month, even slowly.
  • You pay yourself a fixed amount on a fixed date.
