How to use a credit card for business without the debt trap

Leverage that pays you back before the due date, and how to spot the kind that does not.

Educational only, not financial advice. Leverage multiplies losses as well as gains. Figures checked 2026-11-04.

A credit card is the most available form of credit for many Filipino small business owners. No collateral, no long application, and it is already in your wallet. Used well, it gives you a short, free float. Used badly, it becomes the most expensive money you will ever borrow. The difference is almost entirely about timing.

The free float, explained

Every card has a statement date and a due date. Purchases you make right after the statement date will not appear until the next statement, and then you still have the grace period until the due date. Depending on your card, that can add up to roughly 45 to 50 days between swiping and paying. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, you usually pay no interest on regular purchases. That window is the "float".

For a business, the float is useful when you buy something that turns into cash quickly: stock for a confirmed order, ingredients for a weekend bazaar, materials for a job where the client pays on delivery.

The one test that matters

Before you swipe, ask: will this purchase produce cash before the statement due date? Not "probably someday". Real pesos in your account, before the due date, enough to pay the full amount.

  • Yes, with margin to spare: green. The card is a free bridge.
  • Yes, but only if everything goes right: yellow. Have a plan B ready, like a buffer in savings.
  • No, or you are not sure: red. Do not put it on the card.

What it costs when you miss

The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas caps credit card interest or finance charges at 3% per month, or 36% per year, under Circular No. 1165 (2023). Installment loans on cards are capped at a 1% monthly add-on rate, and cash advance processing fees at PHP 200 per transaction. Those are ceilings, not targets, but many cards charge at or near them.

Three percent a month sounds small. On a PHP 50,000 balance it is about PHP 1,500 a month in interest. If you only pay the minimum, most of the payment goes to interest and the balance barely moves. Late fees and a hit to your credit record come on top.

0% installment: when it makes sense

"0% for 12 months" spreads a purchase into equal monthly amortizations. It can work for equipment that earns money every month, like a chest freezer for a sari-sari store or a printer for a print shop, as long as:

  • the item earns more each month than the monthly amortization,
  • you treat the whole amount as already spent from your credit limit, and
  • you read the fine print for processing fees that make it not truly zero.

It does not make sense for things that do not produce cash, like lifestyle purchases charged to the business.

Rewards and cashback: nice, not the reason

Points and cashback are a bonus for spending you already planned. A 1% cashback is wiped out by a single month of interest at 3%. Never spend to chase rewards.

Balance transfers

Some banks offer to move a balance from another card at a lower rate for a fixed term. This can help you escape a 3% monthly balance, but only if you stop adding new debt and you have a payoff plan that ends before the promo does. Check the fees and the rate after the term.

Seven rules for responsible leverage

  1. Only finance purchases with a payback shorter than your float.
  2. Pay the full statement balance every month. Set auto-debit if your bank offers it.
  3. Keep your balance under 30% of your limit.
  4. Never use cash advances for business stock.
  5. Keep personal and business spending on separate cards if you can.
  6. Keep a cash buffer equal to at least one month of card spending.
  7. If you miss a full payment once, stop new card spending until you are back to zero.

A quick example

A food seller buys PHP 30,000 of ingredients for a catering order that pays PHP 39,000 on delivery, 35 days later. The purchase is made right after the statement date, so the due date is about 45 days away. Cash arrives 10 days before the due date. Verdict: green, pay in full, zero interest.

Now the client pays 30 days late instead. Cash arrives on day 65, 20 days past due. At 3% a month, that is roughly PHP 590 in interest, plus any late fee. Still profitable, but much thinner. That is why a yellow verdict needs a buffer.

Bottom line

A credit card is a timing tool, not extra income. Swipe only when the cash comes back first. Everything else is debt with a nice design.
